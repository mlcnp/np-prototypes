I am regularly 😳 shocked by the extreme behaviours in regular classrooms.   There may be different contributing factors, and these trends need further investigation but until then … schools need immediate support. 
  
  Teachers are increasingly facing situations such as:
  
  Repeated classroom evacuations due violent outbursts 
  Destroyed classrooms and materials
  Verbal aggression

  Many students are missing foundational skills that physical education should teach, including:
  Behaviour regulation
  Physical health and fitness
  Social development
  Teamwork
  Understanding rules and boundaries
  Coordination and motor skills
  Basic common sports knowledge
  Too often, PE classes become unstructured free play because there are not enough trained PE teachers, proper facilities, or adequate equipment.
  
  3. Classrooms Lack Tools for Diverse Learners
  Many classrooms do not have the tools many students need to focus, self-regulate, and engage successfully in learning.
  Such as:
  
  Flexible seating
  Standing desks
  Fidgets and sensory tools
  Visual timers and schedules
  Hands-on learning materials beyond photocopies 
  In many cases:
  PACs must fundraise for these resources
  Teachers pay out of pocket
  Parents contribute supplies themselves
  The same issue applies to art, music and shop-style learning activities. All students benefit from practical, hands-on experiences beyond paper-and-pencil tasks, but schools often lack the materials and funding to provide them. 
  
  4. Teachers Need Better Curriculum Support
  
  Project-based learning can be highly effective for engaging diverse and “wiggly” learners, but teachers should not have to constantly imagine and create materials from scratch or purchase unsuitable American Ed templates online.
  teachers need:
  
  Researched and prepared BC curriculum specific resource banks
  Grade-level project-based learning sets  from K to 12. 
  Built-in adaptations for diverse learners, including both emerging and extending students
  
  Without these systems:
  Teachers repeatedly reinvent lessons every year
  Valuable time is lost creating duplicate and often inferior materials
  Students encounter repetitive projects across subjects and grade levels
  For example, the same “food truck” project may appear multiple times in different subjects and years because teachers are independently searching for workable resources.
  
  5. Phonics Instruction Needs to Be Reintroduced and Supported
  
  Foundational reading skills require structured and evidence-based phonics instruction. I don’t know why phonics went away, I don’t even know if parents know it went away. 
  Effective literacy development depends on:
  
  Consistent phonics programs
  Proper teacher training
  High-quality instructional materials
  Clear school-wide implementation
  A stronger system to teach older students who made it to high school with poor reading skills. 
  
  Reading instruction cannot rely on:
  Random worksheets
  Inconsistent teaching methods
  Gaps between classrooms or schools
  Students need systematic support in developing core reading skills.

[Post author 15 · 2026-05-14 22:49 UTC]
  I think the real problem is not that boys and girls are “better” or “worse” than each other. The real problem is that we often expect boys and girls to develop, learn, behave, and succeed in exactly the same way and on the same timeline, when human development is much more complex than that.
  
  Schools are built around certain definitions of success: sitting still for long periods, strong verbal communication, organization, attention regulation, structured work, and standardized testing. Those are valid skills, but they are not the only forms of intelligence, competence, or potential. When one group statistically struggles more within that structure, the conversation immediately becomes “they are falling behind,” instead of asking whether the system is flexible enough to support different developmental needs and learning styles.
  
  That does not mean boys should not be held to standards, nor does it mean girls have an unfair advantage. It means boys and girls are different, and that is okay. Different does not mean unequal in value. Men and women develop differently in some areas, may mature at different rates, may respond differently to the same environment, and may have different strengths or challenges on average. The solution is not to erase those differences or pretend they do not exist. The solution is to better understand them.
  
  That is where I think the article’s wording becomes problematic. Terms like “boy crisis,” “falling behind,” and “systemically disadvantaged” create a very competitive framework. The article repeatedly compares boys’ results directly against girls’ results in areas like graduation rates and academic performance. That subtly pushes readers toward the idea that one sex is “doing better” than the other, when in reality academic metrics only measure a narrow category of performance. They do not measure overall human value, emotional intelligence, resilience, creativity, leadership, social contribution, or success in life as a whole.
  
  The article also mixes educational concerns with broader ideological discussions about “male supremacy” and workplace power structures. That shifts the discussion away from childhood developmental needs and into a social competition between sexes. Once the conversation becomes boys versus girls, everyone loses. Helping boys succeed should not threaten girls, and girls succeeding should not be framed as a problem for boys.
  
  The deeper issue is that we keep trying to force identical outcomes instead of asking better questions:
  Are schools adaptable enough for different developmental styles?
  Are we mistaking quietness and compliance for intelligence?
  Are we measuring true understanding, or mainly the ability to fit one behavioural model?
  Are we supporting children according to their actual needs?
  
  Equality does not always mean treating everyone identically. Equity means recognizing that different students may require different approaches, supports, or environments to have the same opportunity to succeed.
  
  Men and women are not enemies competing for superiority. They complement each other and depend on each other in society, families, workplaces, and communities. The goal should not be to erase differences, rank one sex above the other, or force everyone into the same mold. The goal should be mutual respect, equity, understanding developmental differences, and helping every child reach their full potential.

  There have been several comments so far that I think have captured the essence of the problem - it's not really about gender but about a broken system that, for the most part, has ignored both the socio-cultural realities as well as the science related to both human learning and development.
  
  Many (learned yet exhausted) educators and researchers understand that it's broken.  And it goes well beyond elementary school as the same systemic issues persist through post-secondary education (and even into the workplace). While I'm not 'all in' on the Montessori method, "following the learner" makes a lot of sense given the diversity of youth and their: nature, interests, abilities and proclivities. This is emphasized in adult learning. But as it generally applies to formal education at all levels, based on what I've seen (and lived through with myself, children and grandchildren), there has been a lot of 'admiring the problem' and systemic resistance to introducing a new learning model for education. 
  
  Gender aside, as we look to the future of Canadian society and, indeed humanity, we should be asking core questions like:  
  - What does wholly successful education really look like from the child's and society's perspective (not just the stats but in how outcomes are measured in qualitative terms)?
  - What exactly do all children need to successfully navigate life in the 21st century (acknowledging that much of the current curriculum is based on historical understanding of what formal education is supposed to look like rather than an actual needs-based curriculum)?
  - How can we address the diversity of learning and learner needs to support this success (recognizing the success should not be defined by a credential alone)? And,

[Post author 118 · 2026-05-12 17:22 UTC]
  Boys aren't "falling behind" — our schools are failing to meet their developmental needs.
  
  The research is unequivocal: recess and unstructured outdoor play are not privileges to be earned. They are developmental necessities for all children and most especially for boys.
  
  Boys develop executive function, impulse control, and emotional regulation skills through physical movement and free play. When we remove recess as punishment — which the studies also show disproportionately affects boys — we're actually removing the very tool they need to develop the self-regulation skills we're punishing them for lacking. It's a cruel feedback loop that the system has created.
  Other jurisdictions have figured this out. Finland guarantees 15 minutes of outdoor time after every 45-minute lesson throughout the school day in addition to an long lunch period that also encourages outdoor play.  Finland's PISA scores consistently rank among the world's highest, suggesting their approach works. Japan mandates recess periods and views outdoor play as essential to academic success. Washington State adopted policies ensuring recess cannot be withheld for behavioral reasons, and that outdoor time happens daily except in specified weather conditions. Why? Because the evidence is clear:
  
  - Physical activity directly improves attention, focus, and classroom behavior
  - Unstructured play builds social skills, conflict resolution, and emotional literacy
  - Outdoor time reduces stress hormones and improves mental health
  - Movement supports boys' developmental trajectories
  
  Yet I've watched my son's school routinely remove recess and outdoor lunch time as punishment. A punishment that seems to be reserved for boys only.  For my daughter, I seen the entire school keep all classes inside for weeks on end in the spring because of "rain".  We're sacrificing children's health and development for adult convenience. 
  
  My recommendations:
  - Adopt protected recess policies: Recess is a right, not a reward. Full stop.
  - Increase recess time: it is recommended that elementary students get 60+ minutes daily of unstructured play, yet most of our schools provide 30 minutes or less.
  -Train educators on gender differences in development: Boys' need for movement isn't "bad behavior" — it's biology.
  - Redesign discipline approaches: Removing the tool a child needs to succeed is not discipline. I would argue that it's educational malpractice.
  - Make outdoor time non-negotiable: Weather guidelines should be specific and evidence-based, and published at the School Board level in all communities. This should not be left to adult preference.
  
  Our boys aren't broken. Our system is. And until we acknowledge that sitting still and being quiet for 6+ hours isn't developmentally appropriate for most elementary-aged children — especially boys — we'll continue to see them "fall behind" in schools designed around a narrow definition of success.
  
  The solution isn't to fix the boys. It's to fix the schools.

      Many students are missing foundational skills that physical education should teach, including:
      Behaviour regulation
      Physical health and fitness
      Social development
      Teamwork
      Understanding rules and boundaries
      Coordination and motor skills
      Basic common sports knowledge
      Too often, PE classes become unstructured free play because there are not enough trained PE teachers, proper facilities, or adequate equipment.
      
      3. Classrooms Lack Tools for Diverse Learners
      Many classrooms do not have the tools many students need to focus, self-regulate, and engage successfully in learning.
      Such as:
      
      Flexible seating
      Standing desks
      Fidgets and sensory tools
      Visual timers and schedules
      Hands-on learning materials beyond photocopies 
      In many cases:
      PACs must fundraise for these resources
      Teachers pay out of pocket
      Parents contribute supplies themselves
      The same issue applies to art, music and shop-style learning activities. All students benefit from practical, hands-on experiences beyond paper-and-pencil tasks, but schools often lack the materials and funding to provide them. 
      
      4. Teachers Need Better Curriculum Support
      
      Project-based learning can be highly effective for engaging diverse and “wiggly” learners, but teachers should not have to constantly imagine and create materials from scratch or purchase unsuitable American Ed templates online.
      teachers need:
      
      Researched and prepared BC curriculum specific resource banks
      Grade-level project-based learning sets  from K to 12. 
      Built-in adaptations for diverse learners, including both emerging and extending students
      
      Without these systems:
      Teachers repeatedly reinvent lessons every year
      Valuable time is lost creating duplicate and often inferior materials
      Students encounter repetitive projects across subjects and grade levels
      For example, the same “food truck” project may appear multiple times in different subjects and years because teachers are independently searching for workable resources.
      
      5. Phonics Instruction Needs to Be Reintroduced and Supported
      
      Foundational reading skills require structured and evidence-based phonics instruction. I don’t know why phonics went away, I don’t even know if parents know it went away. 
      Effective literacy development depends on:
      
      Consistent phonics programs
      Proper teacher training
      High-quality instructional materials
      Clear school-wide implementation
      A stronger system to teach older students who made it to high school with poor reading skills. 
      
      Reading instruction cannot rely on:
      Random worksheets
      Inconsistent teaching methods
      Gaps between classrooms or schools
      Students need systematic support in developing core reading skills.
